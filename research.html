Job Market Paper

Wind Integration and Short-Run Market Power in Wholesale Electricity Markets: Evidence from ERCOT

Paper (PDF) ↗

Abstract

Renewable integration can lower wholesale electricity prices by displacing higher-cost conventional generation, but its implications extend beyond the merit-order effect. By reshaping the residual demand faced by conventional generators, variation in wind generation can alter their incentives to exercise market power. Using detailed generator-level bidding and operating data from the Texas electricity market (ERCOT), this paper examines how wind generation affects conventional generators' market power and the resulting welfare implications. I develop a model of strategic bidding that distinguishes static strategic incentives, including residual-demand and interaction effects, from dynamic operating considerations. Even after accounting for dynamic operating conditions, a 1 GW increase in expected wind generation is associated with a $1.38/MWh reduction in conventional generators' bid markups, approximately 30 percent of the average markup. A model-based decomposition identifies changes in residual demand as the primary mechanism underlying the wind–markup relationship. Counterfactual simulations show that a 1 GW increase in wind capacity reduces wholesale electricity prices by $2.48/MWh, including an additional $0.28/MWh reduction from conventional generators' strategic adjustments to their offers beyond the mechanical price effect. While these strategic responses reinforce the price decline, the presence of static market power substantially limits the welfare gains from wind integration. The same capacity expansion raises annual welfare by $289 million under strategic bidding, approximately 54 percent less than the $626 million gain in a benchmark that removes static market power while preserving dynamic operating incentives.
